\begin{abstract}
An iteration may expose a simple direction without exposing a simple law for
its future motion. Discoverable transport concerns the stronger task of
acquiring a representation that can be advanced more cheaply than the
ordinary computation it replaces. We develop this question for mirror,
Bregman, and entropic iterations through three distinct requirements:
closure on the relevant states or requests, acquisition of the information
needed by that closure, and economical evaluation with controlled error.
Finite invariant observables permit exact powering; a trajectory-defect
identity separates compression from evolution of the local geometry.
For positive-kernel Sinkhorn iteration, conditional variance bounds the
nonlinear forcing, and a finite energy budget yields horizon-uniform
first- and second-jet error bounds at a fixed point, without inverse spectral
gap factors. The constants retain their amplitude and marginal dependence.
Exact linear observability and finite joint-pattern algebras give scoped
limits on the dimension required to carry a rule or a primitive response.
These results explain why slow motion, low snapshot rank, and exact
algebraic identities can each coexist with unsuccessful acceleration.
Selected experiments illustrate certified affine-piece discovery, supplied
projective closure, size-dependent primitive reuse, and loss of amortization
through curvature or request-family growth. Primitive reuse improves four of
six reported 1024-point trajectories, but loses on the narrowest kernels
and at smaller size. The resulting theory identifies
sufficient structures and representation-specific obstructions; it does not
assert a universal accelerator or a universal lower bound on acceleration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A slowly changing iterate often suggests that many future steps should be
replaceable by one calculation. The suggestion is incomplete. The direction
may rotate, its rate may depend on unobserved variables, or the representation
needed to carry it may cost more than the steps it replaces. The relevant
question is therefore not only whether motion is predictable, but which
relations make that prediction both valid and economical.

We call this problem \emph{discoverable transport}. Its object is the complete
state of an existing iteration, or the requests made to an expensive primitive
inside that iteration. The representation is acquired from available
expressions, operator actions, and observed states; its validity concerns the
original map. A method that instead reaches the same minimizer by a different
trajectory addresses a related acceleration problem, but does not thereby
establish finite-trajectory transport.

Three distinctions organize the construction. \emph{Closure} determines
whether the retained variables predict what is required next.
\emph{Observability and acquisition} determine whether those variables and
relations can be obtained from the permitted information. \emph{Amortization}
determines whether their entry, composition, validation, and readout costs
are repaid before the representation ceases to be useful. These are
requirements of the transport constructions studied here, not a
classification theorem for every possible acceleration algorithm.

The distinction is visible already in mirror geometry. A dual Jacobian is
self-adjoint in a natural metric, making finite Krylov transport available.
Yet increasing its dimension removes projection error without removing the
error of freezing the geometry. Nonlinear observable coordinates can instead
close the actual dynamics. Their existence depends on the map and the
representation, not on curvature alone. In entropic scaling, the conditional
geometry evolves exactly within a fixed diagonal scaling orbit; carrying
that orbit still need not be cheaper than executing an ordinary pass.

The main results sharpen these observations. We state the exact observable
and approximate path contracts, derive conditional-energy bounds for
Sinkhorn, and separate two finite-dimensional obstructions: the outputs
needed to carry an evolving rule may require more directions than the state,
and pointwise nonlinear requests may generate a much larger algebra than
the linear span of their log directions. These obstructions are explicitly
relative to a representation class. Supplied block and Gaussian families,
certified affine pieces, and measured primitive responses provide positive
counterpoints. The experiments serve to distinguish the theoretical
conditions rather than to enumerate successive implementations.

\section{Relation to Existing Acceleration}
Krylov acceleration is established numerical analysis. Anderson acceleration
on linear problems is related to GMRES~\cite{walker}, nonlinear extrapolation
uses histories of iterates~\cite{scieur}, and GMRES acceleration of quadratic
ADMM has been analyzed~\cite{zhang}. The finite-flow polynomial below is a
classical matrix-function construction~\cite{saadexp}; on a fixed quadratic
its stationary limit is a conjugate-gradient Galerkin correction~\cite{saadbook}.
Neither its polynomial identity nor the application of Krylov methods to
Bregman-related iterations is claimed as new.

Poon and Liang already fit finite companion recurrences to displacement
histories and sum their powers over a finite forecast horizon~\cite{poon2020}.
Their earlier analysis accounts for fitting work and multistep residual
error~\cite{poon2019}. Mai and Johansson accelerate proximal-gradient
iterations in pre-proximal coordinates~\cite{mai}; geometric Bregman
acceleration is also a separate established program~\cite{hanzely}.
The distinction here is between an acquired recurrence, its departure from
the original finite trajectory, and its complete execution cost.

Finite invariant observable spaces belong to Koopman theory~\cite{brunton,ssd}.
QuACK already alternates ordinary optimizer steps with learned forecasts and
loss-based acceptance~\cite{quack}. Invariance diagnostics and multistep
Koopman error bounds are studied directly in~\cite{conradie}. Hidden-variable
memory and delay representations have related formulations in
Koopman--Mori--Zwanzig reduction~\cite{linlu,kamb}. The observable framework
used below is thus a common foundation, not a new name for an undiscovered
linearization principle.

For entropic transport~\cite{peyre}, spectral overrelaxation~\cite{lehmann}
and Arnoldi matrix balancing~\cite{aristodemo} are close algorithmic
comparators. Gaussian Sinkhorn already has finite-dimensional
Kalman/Riccati formulations~\cite{akyildiz}, and Riccati products admit
structured representations~\cite{delmoralhorton}. Our Gaussian example is
an instance of this prior structure. The present contribution is the
connection between geometry-specific defect and energy bounds, the
information required by state-and-rule representations, and the algebra
of primitive requests. A complete comparison with all such accelerators,
specialized transport solvers, or continuation schedules is not supplied.

\section{Closed Representations of an Iteration}
\label{sec:general-geometry}
Let $F:\Omega\to\Omega$ be a deterministic map of the complete state.
Independent splitting memories belong to that state. A quotient is permitted
when it retains everything needed for the specified future output; deletion
of a memory variable without such a property changes the map.

\subsection{Finite observables and the changing derivative}
\begin{proposition}[Finite observable transport]\label{prop:observable}
Suppose $\Phi:\Omega\to\R^r$ and $\chi$ satisfy
\[
 \Phi(F(z))=T\Phi(z),\qquad \chi(\Phi(z))=z
\]
on an invariant set. Then, for every nonnegative integer $H$,
\begin{equation}
 F^H(z)=\chi(T^H\Phi(z)). \label{eq:observable-power}
\end{equation}
If the identities hold on an open set and the maps are differentiable,
\begin{equation}
 D(F^H)(z)=D\chi(T^H\Phi(z))\,T^H D\Phi(z).
\end{equation}
For a smooth invariant manifold the derivative identity applies to its
tangent directions; other perturbations require an entry-map analysis.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Induction gives $\Phi(F^H(z))=T^H\Phi(z)$; decode and differentiate.
\end{proof}
A fixed lifted operator can therefore carry a changing physical derivative.
No explicit matrix of that derivative need be retained. The cyclic minimal
polynomial of $T$ reduces its powers; binary composition evaluates them
without visiting every intermediate state. Entry, decoding, conditioning,
and the arithmetic cost of those powers remain separate obligations.
A finite-dimensional nonlinear parameterization alone does not imply
fixed-size linear powering. In particular a general polynomial rule may
generate successively higher degrees and require an infinite lift.

For example, $u^+=\lambda u$, $v^+=\mu v+\beta u^2$ closes on
$(u,v,u^2)$. At the resonance $\mu=\lambda^2$,
\[
 v_H=\mu^H v_0+H\mu^{H-1}\beta u_0^2.
\]
The resonant interaction is carried by a Jordan block rather than removed
by division by $\mu-\lambda^2$. This special triangular closure does not
extend to arbitrary polynomial maps. The following algebraic condition
identifies a broader sufficient case without assuming that its coordinates
can be acquired cheaply.

\begin{proposition}[Positive weighted-degree closure]\label{prop:weighted}
Let a polynomial map $N:\R^d\to\R^d$ have positive coordinate weights
$w_i>0$, with every monomial in $N_i$ of weighted degree $w_i$.
For any finite $W$, monomials of weighted degree at most $W$ span a finite
space invariant under composition with $N$. If $W\geq\max_iw_i$, that
space contains all state coordinates and gives a finite linear lift.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A monomial $x^\alpha$ has degree $\sum_i\alpha_iw_i$. Substitution of the
weighted-homogeneous $N_i$ preserves that degree term by term. There are
only finitely many nonnegative integer exponents below $W$ because every
$w_i$ is positive. The coordinate monomials are included by the final
hypothesis.
\end{proof}
For a supplied Poincar\'e--Dulac polynomial containing only resonant
monomials and with eigenvalues $0<\lambda_i<1$,
the weights $w_i=-\log\lambda_i$ have this property: a resonant monomial
in coordinate $k$ satisfies $\prod_i\lambda_i^{\alpha_i}=\lambda_k$.
This statement assumes the normal-form coordinates and an applicable local
invariant domain. It proves neither a cheaply discoverable conjugating chart
nor a small lift; the number of admissible monomials can be large. Zero
or unit eigenvalues are outside this positive-finite-weight argument.

\subsection{Compatibility with a prescribed mirror}
Let $\Omega\subset\R^n$ be open and simply connected, let $h\in C^2(\Omega)$
have positive-definite Hessian, and assume $\nabla h$ is injective. Let
$F:\Omega\to\Omega$ be $C^1$ and $\eta>0$. A mirror step for a differentiable
objective satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \nabla h(F(x))=\nabla h(x)-\eta\nabla f(x).
 \label{eq:general-mirror}
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[Geometry--dynamics compatibility]
There exists a $C^2$ objective $f$ satisfying \eqref{eq:general-mirror} if and
only if $H_h(F(x))DF(x)$ is symmetric everywhere. If $\Omega$ is also convex,
that objective is convex if and only if
\begin{equation}
 H_h(x)-H_h(F(x))DF(x)\succeq0 \quad(x\in\Omega).
 \label{eq:general-convexity}
\end{equation}
The objective is unique up to an additive constant on a connected domain.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Define $g(x)=[\nabla h(x)-\nabla h(F(x))]/\eta$. Differentiation gives
\[ Dg(x)=\eta^{-1}[H_h(x)-H_h(F(x))DF(x)]. \]
Since $H_h(x)$ is symmetric,
$Dg$ is symmetric precisely under the stated condition. The closed one-form
$g\cdot dx$ is exact on a simply connected domain, giving $g=\nabla f$.
The converse follows by differentiating \eqref{eq:general-mirror}.
On a convex domain a $C^2$ function is convex precisely when its Hessian is
positive semidefinite. Injectivity of $\nabla h$ identifies the prescribed
$F$ with the mirror update wherever its image lies in $\Omega$.
\end{proof}
This also tests a candidate finite representation: if $\psi$ is an embedding
with decoder $\chi$ and $T\psi(\Omega)\subseteq\psi(\Omega)$, set
$F=\chi T\psi$ and apply the theorem. Algebraic closure alone does not make
the resulting dynamics a gradient method in an arbitrarily assigned mirror.

In dual coordinates $y=\nabla h(x)$, write
$G(y)=\nabla h(F(\nabla h^*(y)))$. The equivalent symmetry test is
\begin{equation}
 DG(y)H_h(x)=H_h(x)DG(y)^T.
 \label{eq:dual-compatibility}
\end{equation}
For an affine dual map $G(y)=Ay+c$, this must hold for the entire family
of mirror Hessians, not just the Hessian at an anchor. For example,
$A=\diag(1/2,4/5)$ passes with $H_h=I$ and fails with
$H_h=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0.6\\0.6&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.
Conversely, every Legendre geometry admits the family
$f=\alpha h-b^Tx$, with $G(y)=(1-\eta\alpha)y+\eta b$ whenever the image
stays in the dual domain. Thus arbitrary curvature can coexist with a
one-mode affine recurrence. More modes require a compatible Hessian family.

These statements refer to a prescribed mirror algorithm in its affine
coordinates. An arbitrary nonlinear coordinate change conjugates an existing
map, but recomputing ordinary mirror descent from a transformed objective
and transformed potential need not produce that conjugate map.


\subsection{Exact families and the knowledge they require}
The simplest family is affine motion. At an anchor $z$, let
$r=F(z)-z$, $J=DF(z)$, and $p_H(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}t^j$. If $F$ is affine
on the visited region, then
\begin{equation}
 F^H(z)-z=p_H(J)r. \label{eq:affine}
\end{equation}
The cyclic space $\K_k(J,r)$ is acquired by operator actions. Exact Arnoldi
closure $JQ=QT$ proves exact transport of this orbit for all horizons
remaining in that affine region. It counts excited eigenvalues and Jordan
structure, rather than ambient dimension. In particular $p_H(1)=H$ carries
constant drift even when no stationary dual solution exists.

Affine pieces of a Huber mirror map supply a \emph{discovered} positive
example. The current feature constraints and cached tangent features give
a first-event horizon and a path certificate (Appendix~\ref{app:huber}).
This is stronger evidence than low snapshot rank: the region of validity
is checked using the known map expression. For the coupled Meyer recurrence,
a faithful quotient retains both vector memories, and disk curvature has
an exact pointwise remainder whose fixed linear response can be bounded
(Appendix~\ref{sec:curved}). An unchanged exterior-disk mask is not an affine
piece, so the Huber argument cannot be reused there.

Nonlinear coordinates give a second route. In a scalar mirror with
$s=h'(q)>0$, prescribe
\[
 s^+=\frac{\alpha s}{1+\beta s},\qquad 0<\alpha\leq1,\quad\beta\geq0.
\]
The objective derivative $f'(q)=s-\alpha s/(1+\beta s)$ has
\[
 f''(q)=\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{(1+\beta s)^2}\right)h''(q)\geq0.
\]
Thus, on an invariant positive dual interval, this is convex mirror descent
and $(1,1/s)$ closes linearly. A rational grammar can identify a
fractional-linear relation and synthesize a cross-ratio coordinate from
its fixed points. The grammar and mirror factorization are supplied;
the fitted relation and its numerical accuracy are learned. Structural
verification, not the fit residual alone, establishes exact closure.

Two-group Sinkhorn provides a \emph{supplied-structure} example within a
standard algorithm. For a positive $2\times2$ kernel, the ratio $v_1/v_2$
obeys a fractional-linear map, hence a $2\times2$ homogeneous matrix carries
both the ratio and its tangent over a finite horizon. A supplied proportional
block partition extends the reduction to large supports
(Appendix~\ref{app:projective}). Rank two by itself does not identify that
partition or certify the reduction.

A continuous Gaussian family provides a different supplied representation.
For centered covariance marginals $A,B\succ0$ and kernel
$\exp(-\norm{x-y}^2/(2t))$, quadratic scaling potentials remain quadratic.
Writing $p\succ0$ for the conditional precision, a complete pass is
\begin{equation}
 p^+=B^{-1}+(t^2A^{-1}+p^{-1})^{-1}. \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
Put $T=t^2A^{-1}$ and $C=B^{-1}$. This is the matrix fractional action
of
\[
 L=\begin{pmatrix}I+CT&C\\T&I\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 p^+=\{(I+CT)p+C\}(Tp+I)^{-1}.
\]
Matrix powers compose the recurrence whenever decoding remains valid.
The dimension is the physical Gaussian dimension, not a discretized support
size. This is established Riccati structure~\cite{akyildiz,delmoralhorton};
Appendix~\ref{app:gaussian} derives the stated convention.
Gaussian closure concerns this preserved quadratic-potential family.
It does not assert vanishing higher cumulants for arbitrary observables:
for a standard Gaussian $X$, the third cumulant of $X^2$ is eight.

\section{Approximate Transport and Geometric Defect}
\label{sec:defects}
Exact closure is a sufficient structure, but a useful finite horizon may
survive a controlled departure from it. The following contract makes the
required departure explicit before discussing acquisition or speed.

\subsection{A path contract and its cost}
For a predicted path $\widehat z_0=z$, let
$d_j=F(\widehat z_j)-\widehat z_{j+1}$. If $F$ is $\ell$-Lipschitz between
the corresponding actual and predicted points, then
\begin{equation}
 \norm{F^H(z)-\widehat z_H}
 \leq\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\ell^{H-1-j}\norm{d_j}.\label{eq:pathcontract}
\end{equation}
Indeed the next error is at most $\ell$ times the current error plus
$\norm{d_j}$. This elementary recursion is useful only when its defects
can be bounded without replaying all the expensive map evaluations.

Let $C_F$ be the cost of one ordinary pass and let
\begin{equation}
 C_{\rm tr}=C_{\rm acquire}+C_{\rm compose}+C_{\rm certify}
             +C_{\rm read}+C_{\rm failed}.\label{eq:totalcost}
\end{equation}
Transport at a specified trajectory tolerance saves time only if
$C_{\rm tr}<HC_F$. This is a cost accounting condition, not a theorem
that any component can be made small. A convergence comparison is different.
If $F$ is a $q$-contraction in one fixed norm, $0<q<1$, and
$\norm{\widehat z_H-F^H(z)}\leq\epsilon\norm{z-z_*}$, then
\[
 \norm{\widehat z_H-z_*}\leq(q^H+\epsilon)\norm{z-z_*}.
\]
With $c=C_{\rm tr}/C_F$, the sufficient worst-case advantage is
$\epsilon<q^c-q^H$. Local metrics at different anchors cannot be substituted
into this comparison without controlling their distortion.

\subsection{Mirror symmetry and frozen finite flow}
Let $\psi$ be a Legendre mirror on an open convex primal domain, and assume
the required derivatives exist with $\nabla^2\psi(x)\succ0$. For smooth $f$,
an interior mirror-descent step with fixed $\alpha>0$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \nabla\psi(x^+)=\nabla\psi(x)-\alpha\nabla f(x).
\end{equation}
With $y=\nabla\psi(x)$ and $x=\nabla\psi^*(y)$, its dual map is
\begin{equation}
 \F(y)=y-\alpha\nabla f(\nabla\psi^*(y)). \label{eq:mirror}
\end{equation}
All coordinates below are genuine local coordinates. On a probability
simplex we use $n-1$ log ratios; the redundant $n$-logit gauge would give a
singular metric.

\begin{proposition}[Local mirror symmetry]\label{prop:symmetry}
At any interior anchor $y$, let
\[
 M=\nabla^2\psi^*(y)=(\nabla^2\psi(x))^{-1},\qquad B=\nabla^2 f(x).
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 J=I-\alpha BM,\qquad MJ=M-\alpha MBM=(MJ)^T. \label{eq:symmetry}
\end{equation}
Hence $J$ is self-adjoint in $\ip{u}{v}_M=u^TMv$. Its similarity transform is
$M^{1/2}JM^{-1/2}=I-\alpha M^{1/2}BM^{1/2}$.
If $\mu\nabla^2\psi(x)\preceq B\preceq L\nabla^2\psi(x)$,
its spectrum lies in $[1-\alpha L,1-\alpha\mu]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiate \eqref{eq:mirror}. Symmetry follows because both $B$ and $M$
are symmetric. Congruencing the relative Hessian bounds by $M^{1/2}$ gives
the spectral statement. No fixed-point condition was used.
\end{proof}

The statement supplies a principled inner product for Arnoldi. In exact
arithmetic its reduced matrix is symmetric, so a Lanczos implementation is
available. The implementations use twice-reorthogonalized Arnoldi for numerical
robustness, charging its full cost. Applying $M$ can itself be expensive;
the result is practically useful only when that action is available at an
acceptable cost. Diagonal quadratic and separable entropy metrics provide inexpensive
actions; general dense mirror metrics need not.

Fix an SPD metric $M$ at the anchor and write $\beta=\norm{r}_M$.
If $r=0$, the anchor is unchanged. Otherwise construct an $M$-orthonormal
basis of
\[
 \K_k(J,r)=\spanop\{r,Jr,\ldots,J^{k-1}r\}.
\]
Let $Q^TMQ=I$, $Qe_1=r/\beta$, and $T_r=Q^TMJQ$. The proposal is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat z_H=z+\delta_{k,H},\qquad
 \delta_{k,H}=\beta Qp_H(T_r)e_1. \label{eq:proposal}
\end{equation}
Only actions of $J$ are required. The reduced polynomial is evaluated by
$c_0=0$, $c_{j+1}=\beta e_1+T_r c_j$, $\delta_{k,H}=Qc_H$.
This recurrence does not invert $I-T_r$ and remains well-defined for unit
eigenvalues and Jordan blocks. For large horizons, the pair
$(T_r^H,p_H(T_r))$ also admits binary composition, so reduced evaluation need
not visit every intermediate state.

Let $M$ be fixed during one block, let $Q,T_r$ be the computed projection, and
write $E=JQ-QT_r$. For exact Arnoldi $E=h_{k+1,k}q_{k+1}e_k^T$; using the
full residual matrix also accounts for floating-point orthogonalization
error. Define
\[
 c_0=0,\quad c_{j+1}=\beta e_1+T_r c_j,\quad
 v_j=Qc_j,\quad \widehat z_j=z+v_j.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Discrete trajectory-defect identity]\label{thm:defect}
If all required evaluations are in the domain of $\F$, define
\[
 N(v)=\F(z+v)-\F(z)-Jv.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 d_j:=\F(\widehat z_j)-\widehat z_{j+1}
     =N(v_j)+Ec_j. \label{eq:defect}
\end{equation}
If $\F$ is $\ell$-Lipschitz in the fixed $M$-norm between corresponding
actual and predicted points, then
\begin{equation}
 \norm{\F^H(z)-\widehat z_H}_M
 \leq\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\ell^{H-1-j}
       \big(\norm{N(v_j)}_M+\norm{Ec_j}_M\big). \label{eq:defectbound}
\end{equation}
If the derivative is $L_J$-Lipschitz in the induced $M$-norm on each segment
from $z$ to $z+v_j$, then $\norm{N(v_j)}_M\leq\tfrac{L_J}{2}\norm{v_j}_M^2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Use $r=\beta Qe_1$ to subtract the reduced recurrence from
$\F(z+v_j)=z+r+Jv_j+N(v_j)$, giving \eqref{eq:defect}.
For $a_j=\F^j(z)-\widehat z_j$,
$a_{j+1}=\F(\F^j(z))-\F(\widehat z_j)+d_j$ and $a_0=0$.
Apply the Lipschitz bound recursively. The derivative-remainder estimate
follows by integrating along the segment.
\end{proof}

Increasing the Krylov dimension addresses $Ec_j$. It cannot in general
remove $N(v_j)$: a larger exact representation of the wrong frozen geometry
still predicts the wrong trajectory. At a nonsmooth crossing,
\eqref{eq:defect} remains an algebraic identity for a selected linear action,
but the quadratic remainder estimate need not hold. Evaluating every $d_j$
requires the very map calls one hopes to avoid. Direct defect evaluation
therefore serves as a separately costed diagnostic; it cannot be treated
as a free runtime certificate.

\subsection{Relating the metric error to a Bregman distance}
For $x_a=\nabla\psi^*(y_a)$ and $x_b=\nabla\psi^*(y_b)$,
\[
 D_\psi(x_a,x_b)=D_{\psi^*}(y_b,y_a).
\]
If the Hessian along the dual segment is bounded by
$aM\preceq\nabla^2\psi^*\preceq bM$, then
\begin{equation}
 \tfrac a2\norm{y_b-y_a}_M^2
 \leq D_\psi(x_a,x_b)
 \leq\tfrac b2\norm{y_b-y_a}_M^2.
\end{equation}
This translates the fixed-metric transport error into the original mirror
geometry when the metric distortion is controlled. It also identifies a
limitation near domain boundaries where the required constants may degenerate.


\section{Conditional Energy in Entropic Transport}
\label{sec:entropic-evolution}
A long relaxation time does not by itself imply large accumulated nonlinear
forcing. In Sinkhorn iteration, the same conditional geometry controls the
linear dissipation and the curvature. This yields a quantitative distinction
between a small spectral gap and failure of an approximate transport model.

Let $K\in\R_{>0}^{m\times n}$ and let $a,b$ be positive marginals of equal
total mass. All exponentials, logarithms, and vector quotients below are
componentwise. In log column-scaling coordinates, one full pass is
\begin{equation}
 x(y)=\log a-\log(Ke^y),\qquad
 F(y)=\log b-\log(K^Te^{x(y)}).
 \label{eq:entropic-map}
\end{equation}
The next row scaling is determined by $y$ and carries no independent memory
in this ordinary algorithm. This elimination is specific to its phase
contract; it does not justify deleting independent splitting memories.
The map has the gauge symmetry $F(y+\gamma\mathbf1)=F(y)+\gamma\mathbf1$.

Set $u=e^{x(y)}$, $v=e^y$, and define
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 \Pi&=\diag(u)K\diag(v),\qquad c=\Pi^T\mathbf1,\\
 P&=\diag(a)^{-1}\Pi,\qquad Q=\diag(c)^{-1}\Pi^T.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:entropic-conditionals}\end{equation}
Thus $\Pi\mathbf1=a$, while $c$ is the current column marginal. Both
$P$ and $Q$ are row stochastic.

\begin{proposition}[Moving conditional metric]
\label{prop:entropic-metric}
At every finite state,
\begin{equation}
 J(y)=DF(y)=QP,\qquad
 \diag(c)J=\Pi^T\diag(a)^{-1}\Pi.
 \label{eq:entropic-metric}
\end{equation}
Consequently $J$ is self-adjoint and positive semidefinite in the
$c$-weighted inner product, with spectrum in $[0,1]$. For the actual
displacement $d=F(y)-y$,
\begin{equation}
 c=b\odot e^{-d}. \label{eq:entropic-metric-displacement}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Differentiation gives $Dx[h]=-Ph$ and $DF[h]=QPh$.
The second identity in \eqref{eq:entropic-metric} follows by multiplication.
Conjugating $J$ by $\diag(\sqrt c)$ gives $S^TS$, where
$S=\diag(a)^{-1/2}\Pi\diag(c)^{-1/2}$. Jensen's inequality and the two
marginal identities imply
$\norm{Ph}_a\leq\norm h_c$ and $\norm{Qk}_c\leq\norm k_a$.
The spectrum therefore lies in $[0,1]$. Finally,
$c=v\odot(K^Tu)$ and $e^{F(y)}=b/(K^Tu)$ imply
\eqref{eq:entropic-metric-displacement}.
\end{proof}
For the remainder of this section the marginals have unit mass. The metric
$c$ is determined by the actual displacement, but the full operator $J$ is
not. Under a displacement $h$, put $e=-\log(Pe^h)$. Direct normalization gives
\begin{align}
 P^+&=\diag(Pe^h)^{-1}P\diag(e^h),\nonumber\\
 Q^+&=\diag(Qe^e)^{-1}Q\diag(e^e),\label{eq:conditional-evolution}\\
 F(y+h)-F(y)&=-\log(Qe^e).\nonumber
\end{align}
These identities close the evolving geometry in a diagonal scaling orbit.
They do not reduce its dense application cost. Nor does instantaneous
self-adjointness imply that operators at successive anchors commute.

\subsection{Curvature is controlled by conditional variance}
Write $\Gamma_y(u,v)=D^2F(y)[u,v]/2$ and define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal E_y(h)=\sum_i a_i\Var_{P_i}(h)
               =\ip{h}{(I-J)h}_c.\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Let $\norm{h}_{c,p}$ denote the probability-weighted $L^p$ norm and
$\osc(h)=\max h-\min h$. The latter is the norm on log states modulo
the constant gauge.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional curvature and finite remainder]\label{thm:energy}
At every finite anchor,
\begin{equation}
 \norm{\Gamma_y(u,v)}_{c,1}
 \leq\sqrt{\mathcal E_y(u)\mathcal E_y(v)}.\label{eq:bilinear-energy}
\end{equation}
For $R=\osc(h)$, define $C_2(R)=2\phi_2(2R)$ and
$C_3(R)=5R\phi_3(2R)$, where
\[
 \phi_k(s)=\frac{e^s-\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}s^j/j!}{s^k},
 \qquad \phi_k(0)=1/k!.
\]
Then
\begin{align}
 \norm{F(y+h)-F(y)-Jh}_{c,1}
 &\leq C_2(R)\mathcal E_y(h),\label{eq:linear-energy}\\
 \norm{F(y+h)-F(y)-Jh-\Gamma_y(h,h)}_{c,1}
 &\leq C_3(R)\mathcal E_y(h).\label{eq:cubic-energy}
\end{align}
These are finite-amplitude bounds at the given anchor; no fixed point
or spectral separation is assumed.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Differentiating conditional means gives
\[
 2\Gamma_y(u,v)=Q\Cov_P(u,v)-\Cov_Q(Pu,Pv).
\]
Conditional Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the first term in weighted $L^1$ by
$\sqrt{\mathcal E_y(u)\mathcal E_y(v)}$. For the second, use
\[
 \sum_jc_j\Var_{Q_j}(Pu)=\ip{u}{(J-J^2)u}_c
 \leq\mathcal E_y(u),
\]
and the same inequality for $v$. Division by two proves
\eqref{eq:bilinear-energy}.

Along $y_s=y+sh$, exponential tilting gives
$e^{-sR}\leq(P_s)_{ij}/P_{ij}\leq e^{sR}$ and the same comparison for
$(c_s)_j/c_j$. Evaluating tilted variances about the old conditional mean
yields $\mathcal E_{y_s}(h)\leq e^{sR}\mathcal E_y(h)$.
Consequently $\norm{D^2F(y_s)[h,h]}_{c,1}
\leq2e^{2sR}\mathcal E_y(h)$.

With $\kappa_3$ the third central moment, a further derivative is
\begin{align*}
 D^3F(y)[h,h,h]={}&Q\kappa_{3,P}(h)\;+
                  \kappa_{3,Q}(Ph)\\
                &-3\Cov_Q(Ph,\Var_P(h)).
\end{align*}
The bounds $|\kappa_3(X)|\leq\operatorname{range}(X)\Var(X)$ and
$|\Cov(X,V)|\leq\operatorname{range}(X)\mathbb E V$ for $V\geq0$
give $5R\mathcal E_y(h)$ in the current weighted $L^1$ norm.
The two tilt comparisons give $5Re^{2sR}\mathcal E_y(h)$ in the anchor
norm. Taylor's integral remainder proves both finite bounds.
\end{proof}
For a normalized eigenvector $Jv=\lambda v$,
$\mathcal E_y(v)=1-\lambda$. Thus slow directions have small quadratic
forcing in the stated norms. This coefficientwise fact is not an analytic
linearization radius or a guarantee that a single slow coordinate closes.
Mixed forcing can still carry information from other directions.

\subsection{A finite energy budget over arbitrary horizons}
Positive linear maps and their log forms preserve order; the full $F$ is
order preserving and translation equivariant. Hence $F$ is nonexpansive in
oscillation. This supplies the common norm needed to accumulate defects.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-point finite-energy transport]\label{thm:uniform}
Assume $F(y_*)=y_*$, set $c=b$, $m=\min_jc_j>0$, and let $h$ be
$c$-centered, fixing the constant gauge used to measure amplitude. Put $A=\norm{h}_{c,2}$, $R=\osc(h)$, $J=DF(y_*)$,
and $\Gamma=\Gamma_{y_*}$. Define
\begin{align*}
 \ell_0=h,\quad q_0=0,\qquad
 \ell_{j+1}&=J\ell_j,\\
 q_{j+1}&=Jq_j+\Gamma(\ell_j,\ell_j).
\end{align*}
For every finite $H$,
\begin{equation}
 \osc\{F^H(y_*+h)-y_*-J^Hh\}
 \leq C_2(R)A^2/m.\label{eq:uniform-linear}
\end{equation}
With $S=A^2/\sqrt m$ and $\bar R=R+A^2/m$,
\begin{align}
 &\osc\{F^H(y_*+h)-y_*-\ell_H-q_H\}\nonumber\\
 &\hspace{6mm}\leq
 \frac{2AS+S^2+C_3(\bar R)(A+S)^2}{m}.\label{eq:uniform-jet}
\end{align}
Uniformity is in $H$ at fixed amplitude and positive marginal geometry.
The bounds contain no inverse spectral gap; they do contain $m^{-1}$,
$R$, and an unknown fixed-point displacement. For fixed $c$, the second
bound is $O(A^3)$ as $A\to0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Self-adjointness and $0\preceq J\preceq I$ give
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\mathcal E(J^jh)
 \leq\norm h_{c,2}^2=A^2,\label{eq:energy-budget}
\end{equation}
since $\mathcal E(w)\leq\norm w_{c,2}^2-\norm{Jw}_{c,2}^2$.
Also $\osc(J^jh)\leq R$. Apply \eqref{eq:linear-energy} to each
linear-path defect, use $\osc(w)\leq\norm w_{c,1}/m$, and accumulate by
nonexpansivity to obtain \eqref{eq:uniform-linear}.

Set $f_j=\Gamma(\ell_j,\ell_j)$. Equations
\eqref{eq:bilinear-energy} and \eqref{eq:energy-budget} imply
$\sum_j\norm{f_j}_{c,1}\leq A^2$, whence
$\osc(q_j)\leq A^2/m$. The impulse expansion of $q_j$, Minkowski's
inequality in the space of energy sequences, and
$\norm f_{c,2}\leq\norm f_{c,1}/\sqrt m$ give
\[
 \left(\sum_j\mathcal E(q_j)\right)^{1/2}
 \leq\sum_j\norm{f_j}_{c,2}\leq S.
\]
For $z_j=\ell_j+q_j$, therefore,
$\osc(z_j)\leq\bar R$ and
$\sum_j\mathcal E(z_j)\leq(A+S)^2$.
The quadratic discrepancy has total $L^1(c)$ norm at most
$2AS+S^2$ by \eqref{eq:bilinear-energy}. The remaining Taylor defects
sum to at most $C_3(\bar R)(A+S)^2$. Converting to oscillation and
using nonexpansivity proves \eqref{eq:uniform-jet}.
\end{proof}
This second jet is the linear evolution on
$(z_j,\ell_j\otimes\ell_j)$ with block operator
\[
 \mathcal L=\begin{pmatrix}J&\Gamma_{\rm flat}\\0&J\otimes J\end{pmatrix}.
\]
It retains resonant interactions. It is not repeated application of the
quadratic Taylor map, whose higher-degree terms would evolve differently.
The uncompressed tensor dimension is itself a substantial cost.

At a general anchor, a proposed path $s_j$ instead has the defect
\[
 \eta_j=\osc\{s_{j+1}-r-Js_j-\Gamma_y(s_j,s_j)\},\quad r=F(y)-y.
\]
The same argument gives a \emph{pathwise} bound
\begin{align}
 &\osc\{F^H(y+s_0)-y-s_H\}\nonumber\\
 &\quad\leq\sum_{j<H}\left[
 \eta_j+\frac{C_3(\osc s_j)\mathcal E_y(s_j)}{\min c}\right].
 \label{eq:moving-path}
\end{align}
It is not a proved horizon-uniform extension of Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform}.
If $s_j=C L^j\xi$, the energy sum can be composed by a Gramian:
\begin{align*}
 G_H&=\sum_{j<H}(L^j)^TW L^j,\qquad
 W=C^T\diag(c)(I-J)C,\\
 P_{a+b}&=P_bP_a,\qquad G_{a+b}=G_a+P_a^TG_bP_a,
 \quad P_a=L^a.
\end{align*}
Binary composition costs $O(r^3\log H)$ for an $r$-dimensional dense lift.
A path-radius bound and the model-defect sum are still needed. Recovering
$h$ from an observed residual through $(I-J)^{-1}$ can reintroduce inverse-gap
sensitivity. The energy theorem removes a forcing obstruction, not the
acquisition problem.

\section{What Must Be Observed and Retained}
\label{sec:observability}
A narrow state trajectory need not determine its changing local rule.
The distinction can be stated exactly for linearized output prediction
and then tested without converting it into a universal impossibility claim.

\begin{proposition}[Exact linear rule observability]\label{prop:observability}
For $h^+=Jh$ and required output $Oh$, the least dimension of an exact
linear autonomous representation
\[
 \xi=Rh,\qquad \xi^+=T\xi,\qquad Oh=D_o\xi
\]
valid for every $h\in\R^n$ is
\begin{equation}
 r_* = \dim\spanop\operatorname{rows}
           \{O,OJ,\ldots,OJ^{n-1}\}.\label{eq:obsrank}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The identities $RJ=TR$, $O=D_oR$ force the row space of $R$ to be
$J$-invariant and to contain every displayed row. Conversely that row span
is invariant by Cayley--Hamilton; a basis supplies $R$, $T$, and $D_o$.
\end{proof}
To carry selected changing-rule entries, include in $O$ the functionals
$h\mapsto2\ip{v_i}{\Gamma_y(h,v_j)}_c$, not just the retained state coordinates.
Thus adding rule outputs can increase $r_*$. Eliminating hidden variables
also creates temporal memory: if $x^+=Ax+Bz$, $z^+=Cx+Dz$, then
\[
 x_{k+1}=Ax_k+BD^kz_0+
       \sum_{j<k}BD^{k-1-j}Cx_j.
\]
Delay coordinates may represent that information; omission does not make it
disappear~\cite{linlu,kamb}.

\subsection{A tolerance-dependent directional law}
Let $T_H=[O^T,(OJ)^T,\ldots,(OJ^{H-1})^T]^T$ and let the perturbation ensemble have
second-moment matrix $\Sigma=\mathbb E[hh^T]$. In specified input and output coordinates put
$M_H=\Sigma^{1/2}T_H^TT_H\Sigma^{1/2}$, with eigenvalues
$\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq\cdots$. If $\tr M_H>0$, the best rank-$r$
linear stacked predictor has relative mean-square loss
\begin{equation}
 1-\frac{\sum_{i\leq r}\lambda_i}{\tr M_H}.
 \label{eq:directionlaw}
\end{equation}
This is the singular-value truncation theorem applied to $T_H\Sigma^{1/2}$.
In particular its captured fraction is at most $r/d_{\rm eff}$, where
$d_{\rm eff}=\tr M_H/\lambda_1$. Capture $\eta$ requires
$r\geq\eta d_{\rm eff}$ within this model class.

The law is exact for the specified linear prediction task and perturbation
ensemble. It is a ratio of mean squared errors, not a worst-case relative
bound. A 99\% energy capture permits 10\% relative RMS error. Moreover the
optimal stacked predictor need not admit autonomous projected dynamics:
a Gramian tail bound does not certify invariance or control nonlinear
regeneration of discarded modes. Neither mixture count nor ambient dimension
can replace this task-dependent spectrum without another theorem.

Finite observations alone also cannot certify unrestricted smooth future
maps. A smooth perturbation supported away from the finitely queried states
leaves every observed value and derivative unchanged and can change a later
unqueried transition. Certification consequently needs a stated structural
class, an invariant-region bound, or another source of information beyond
snapshot rank. This does not forbid useful statistical prediction.

\subsection{The algebra of nonlinear requests}
There is a complementary way to avoid carrying an evolving Jacobian.
Sinkhorn repeatedly requests $Kv$ and $K^Tu$, while its reciprocals remain
cheap full-vector operations. The linear relation $K$ is fixed even when
its normalized conditional geometry changes. Its action on a retained
request space can be measured once and reused.

\begin{theorem}[Finite joint-pattern algebra]\label{thm:patterns}
Let $w_1,\ldots,w_d\in\R^n$. Partition the indices by equality of the
joint tuples $(w_1(i),\ldots,w_d(i))$, and let $N_{\rm pat}$ be the number of
nonempty classes. The unital pointwise algebra generated by the $w_j$
is precisely the space of vectors constant on those classes; its dimension
is $N_{\rm pat}$. It is closed under componentwise exponentiation and reciprocals
of vectors with no zero coordinate. If $Q$ spans this algebra and $x_0>0$,
measured columns of $K\diag(x_0)Q$ evaluate every request
\[
 K\left[x_0\odot\exp\!\left(\sum_j s_jw_j\right)\right]
\]
by linear combination.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Every generated product is constant on each class. Choose a linear
combination $w=\sum_j\alpha_jw_j$ separating the finitely many distinct
tuples; the forbidden coefficients form finitely many hyperplanes.
Univariate Lagrange polynomials in $w$ give the indicator of each class.
These indicators span all class-constant vectors, proving equality and the
dimension claim. Any componentwise function defined on the finitely many
values remains class-constant. Linearity of $K$ proves the response claim.
\end{proof}
For binary generators $N_{\rm pat}\leq2^d$; a single generator with $n$ distinct
values already has $N_{\rm pat}=n$. Thus a small span of log directions need not
produce a small exact algebra of requests. This is an obstruction to
\emph{exact linear-space closure on the full generated family}. It is not
a lower bound for approximate representations, a restricted path, nonlinear
parameterizations, or every acceleration method. It also does not prove that
ordinary Sinkhorn remains in a chosen generator family. That membership
must be established or checked at every actual request.

\subsection{Admission and numerical transport of primitive responses}
At a positive anchor $x_0$ let $k_0=Kx_0$ and
$P_0=\diag(k_0)^{-1}K\diag(x_0)$. For an actual request $x>0$, write
$v=x/x_0=Qc+r$ and $\widehat p=P_0Qc$. Positivity yields
\begin{equation}
 \eta=\max_i|r_i|/v_i<1
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 |P_0v-\widehat p|\leq\eta P_0v.\label{eq:primitive-admit}
\end{equation}
The represented output is positive and its Hilbert log-distance from the
exact one is at most $\log((1+\eta)/(1-\eta))$.
Positive linear maps are nonexpansive in that distance, while reciprocals
and fixed positive diagonal scalings preserve it. Summing the admitted
half-pass errors bounds departure from the original ordinary trajectory.

This gate uses the full observed input, not a prediction of what the next
request ought to be. If each stored response has a proved componentwise
error matrix $E$, the additional output uncertainty is bounded by
$E|c|$; otherwise \eqref{eq:primitive-admit} covers exact arithmetic only.
The numerical results use double precision with roundoff allowances and
independent comparisons against direct evaluation. These checks do not
constitute directed-rounding interval certification.

At another anchor $x_1$ with known $k_1=Kx_1$, measured pairs transform as
\begin{align*}
 P'_0&=\diag(k_0/k_1)P_0\diag(x_1/x_0),\\
 Q'&=\diag(x_0/x_1)Q,\qquad P'_0Q'=\diag(k_0/k_1)P_0Q.
\end{align*}
This is an exact change of representation. Its input diagonal condition
number is $\exp(\osc\log(x_1/x_0))$; near-dependent basis transformations
can further amplify response errors. Stable frame transfer, useful future
request coverage, and cheap acquisition are therefore different questions.
Exact algebra settles none of their numerical cost comparisons by itself.

\subsection{Where a relation becomes an executable shortcut}
The Huber example acquires the drift, its relation, and the first feature
event from the current state. At $n=256$, three positive-sensing instances
admitted initial exact-arithmetic horizons 838, 831, and 821 with one map
and one tangent action; independent replay errors were at most
$3.7\cdot10^{-11}$ in absolute whitened dual norm. The complete-solve
speedup in Table~\ref{tab:regimes} is a separate measurement, including
later failed acquisitions and checks. At smaller size or with frequent
feature events, those costs were not repaid.

The proportional-block example instead begins with the invariant family
supplied. Both methods aggregate the same inputs, evolve the same reduced
map, and reconstruct the full scalings. For coupling $.001$, ordinary
reduced runs took about 14--15 ms while projective powers and readout took
$.19$--$.26$ ms. This isolates economical composition after entry; it
cannot be used as evidence that arbitrary kernels reveal such a partition.

The rational cross-ratio control occupies an intermediate position. Eight
transitions supply observations in known mirror coordinates; a finite
rational grammar identifies the chart. Across four mirror geometries and
three seeds, the maximum relative primal error at horizon 128 was
$2.88\cdot10^{-11}$. This verifies a favorable representation-discovery
mechanism, not a general grammar or a repeated wall-clock performance gate.

\subsection{Small forcing does not establish cheap acquisition}
The conditional-energy study checked 50 finite remainder bounds and 28
fixed-point horizon/amplitude cases. All finite bounds held in those
floating-point checks. As a controlled spectral gap decreased from $.386$
to $.00145$, the horizon $\lceil3/\mathrm{gap}\rceil$ grew from 8 to 2070.
At amplitude $.1$, maximum second-jet error changed from
$1.13\cdot10^{-4}$ to $1.17\cdot10^{-4}$. At the smallest gap, halving
amplitude twice reduced errors to $1.46\cdot10^{-5}$ and
$1.82\cdot10^{-6}$, consistent with the cubic bound rather than an
inverse-gap blowup.

The bound itself can nevertheless be unusable. Replicating every atom 32
times, from three states to 96, preserves the physical conditional action
but worsens the coarse marginal-dependent certificate by approximately
1600-fold. The experiment diagnoses the $\min c$ conversion; it does not
provide a proved discretization-independent replacement. Likewise, the
fixed-point displacement and full second jet are scoring objects in this
study, not free runtime information.

The observability tests expose a separate limitation. At $n=128$, the
four-by-four reference rule block required 12--23 directions to retain
99.9\% of its frozen observability-Gramian trace, whereas its four state
coordinates alone require four. These are trace-capture ranks, not
worst-case relative-error certificates. At early anchors of the two
$\varepsilon=.001$ cases, more than 32 and 28 current leading modes were
needed to capture 99\% of the fixed point's leading direction; both late
anchors needed one. This is evidence against a uniformly tiny leading
cluster over that acquisition window, not against every other representation.

History was not categorically uninformative. An order-eight recurrence,
given a favorable oracle signal of frozen rule drift, predicted 32 held-out
values to within 3\% relative RMS error in 21 of 24 cases with relative
noise $10^{-6}$. The fixed point and exact modal signal were supplied to
this diagnostic. It sets a target for causal acquisition; it is not an
implemented general acquisition guarantee.

\subsection{Pattern closure and its loss under richer requests}
The joint-pattern control uses 256 positive requests on 128 coordinates,
two seeds, and a 64-column budget. For supplied binary fields with one,
two, and four directions, acquisition and all queries cost 2, 4, and 16
kernel-vector equivalents. Learning directions from the first eight live
log requests costs 9, 11, and 23--27 equivalents. All subsequent requests
were admitted. Separate powers of the original directions fail in the
multidirection cases, demonstrating the role of mixed products.

With continuous Gaussian fields, supplied one-direction representations
still admitted all subsequent requests. Two directions cost 279 and 266
products, exceeding the ordinary 256; four cost 319 in both seeds and
admitted none. Total log-coefficient RMS was held fixed; maximum log range
was not. These results do not establish a universal law in Gaussianity.
The precise exact-family statement is Theorem~\ref{thm:patterns};
approximate cost additionally depends on range, tolerance, conditioning,
and the portion of the family actually visited.

On the difficult ordinary Sinkhorn trajectories at $n=1024$,
$\varepsilon=.001$, two learned directions required 1016 products per
seed for 256 passes, against the ordinary 512. Four directions required
1015 and 1012, admitting only one and four reuses. Acquisition consumed
nearly all the extra work. After rejection, the already-required full
product audited the rejected prediction: none of the 496 rejected
predictions per seed in the two-direction case had componentwise relative
output error below $10^{-8}$. Only two of 495 and one of 492 did so with
four directions. A looser gate would therefore not repair the dominant
representation failure. These diagnostics do not isolate generator
insufficiency from polynomial truncation.

Frame conditioning is distinct again. Transferring measured responses
through repeated ill-conditioned basis changes initially corrupted their
images. Carrying response uncertainty, spectral rebasing, and immutable
physical action pairs repaired the tested accuracy but still lost complete
cost. The algebraic frame identity does not guarantee either numerical
stability or future request coverage.

\section{Conditions for Plausible Acceleration}
A transport representation is promising when the required outputs close in
a space that can be acquired before its validity expires, and when its
composition and validation are cheaper than the work replaced. The theory
gives several concrete ways this can happen: a discovered affine piece
with a distant event, a supplied finite invariant family with cheap entry,
a controlled finite-energy path with an economical certificate, or a
sequence of actual requests well covered by measured primitive responses.
These mechanisms carry different objects and need different evidence.

The corresponding failures are equally distinct. A small frozen space may
miss changing geometry; a geometrically narrow path may hide rule memory;
a small set of log directions may generate a large multiplicative family;
and a mathematically exact frame may be numerically expensive or unstable.
Equation~\eqref{eq:directionlaw} provides a sharp directional law for a
specified linear output problem. Theorem~\ref{thm:patterns} supplies an
exact dimension law for a specified pointwise algebra. Neither establishes
an intrinsic scalar complexity law for arbitrary mixture models or all
forms of acceleration.

The finite-energy theorem explains why slow relaxation alone need not
defeat approximate transport: its weak dissipation also limits accumulated
nonlinear forcing. The unresolved step is obtaining and advancing the
required information economically at accessible anchors. Small Gramian tails,
accurate local fits, and valid but conservative bounds each address only
part of that step. A practical theory must retain their acquisition and
conditioning constants alongside their approximation rates.

Discoverable transport is therefore a question about relations preserved
by computation. Exact families show that substantial shortcuts are possible;
scoped obstructions show why the same representation need not extend to a
richer family. The empirical outcomes locate examples on both sides without
turning a failed carrier into a general impossibility theorem, or a successful
supplied family into a universal discovery procedure.

\section{Quadratic Finite Flow and Its Classical Content}
\label{app:cg}
This section identifies precisely the classical linear-algebraic mechanism.
Let $\psi(x)=\tfrac12x^TGx$, $G\succ0$, and
$f(x)-f(x_*)=\tfrac12(x-x_*)^TB(x-x_*)$, $B\succ0$.
Then $y=Gx$, $M=G^{-1}$, and $J=I-\alpha BM$ are constant.
Assume the eigenvalues of $G^{-1/2}BG^{-1/2}$ lie in $[\mu,L]$ and
$0<\alpha\leq 1/L$. Set
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 q&=1-\alpha\mu,\quad \kappa=L/\mu,\quad
 \theta=\frac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1},\\
 W&=M(I-J)=\alpha MBM.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:quadparams}\end{equation}
The squared $W$-norm of the dual error equals $2\alpha$ times the objective gap.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-horizon Galerkin contraction]\label{thm:cg}
In exact arithmetic, the natural-metric proposal satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \frac{f(\widehat x_H)-f(x_*)}{f(x)-f(x_*)}
 \leq q^{2H}+(1-q^{2H})\min\{1,4\theta^{2k}\},
 \label{eq:cgbound}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat x_H=G^{-1}\widehat z_H$.
More precisely, define the stationary Galerkin correction
\[
 \delta_\infty=\beta Q(I-T_r)^{-1}e_1,
 \qquad e_\infty=y+\delta_\infty-y_*.
\]
Then the finite-horizon error has the orthogonal decomposition
\begin{equation}
 \norm{y+\delta_{k,H}-y_*}_W^2
 =\norm{e_\infty}_W^2+
   \norm{\delta_{k,H}-\delta_\infty}_W^2. \label{eq:orthogonal}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $S=I-J$, $e=y-y_*$. Since $r=-Se$ and
$\K_k(J,r)=\K_k(S,r)$, $\delta_\infty$ is the $M$-Galerkin solution of
$S\delta=r$. Thus $Q^TWe_\infty=0$. Both $\delta_{k,H}$ and
$\delta_\infty$ lie in $\operatorname{ran}Q$, proving
\eqref{eq:orthogonal}. The reduced matrix $T_r$ is symmetric with spectrum
in $[0,q]$, while $Q^TWQ=I-T_r$. Consequently
\begin{align*}
 \delta_\infty-\delta_{k,H}&=\beta QT_r^H(I-T_r)^{-1}e_1,\\
 \norm{\delta_\infty-\delta_{k,H}}_W&\leq q^H\norm{\delta_\infty}_W.
\end{align*}
Galerkin orthogonality also gives
$\norm{e}_W^2=\norm{e_\infty}_W^2+\norm{\delta_\infty}_W^2$.
Combine these identities with the classical CG polynomial bound
\cite{saadbook},
$\norm{e_\infty}_W\leq 2\theta^k\norm{e}_W$,
and with its trivial bound $\norm{e_\infty}_W\leq\norm{e}_W$.
Converting squared energy to objective gap proves \eqref{eq:cgbound}.
\end{proof}

This theorem does not establish a competitor to CG on a fixed quadratic.
The stationary Galerkin correction has no larger energy error than its
finite-horizon truncation. The finite polynomial becomes interesting when
the geometry changes, a finite trajectory is itself the target, or a
stationary local equation is ill-conditioned or inconsistent. On an affine
quadratic, classical CG is a necessary reference point.


\section{Affine-Piece Discovery and Event Horizons}\label{app:huber}
A class with stronger available structure permits actual certification.
Consider the Huber inverse problem
\[
 f(x)=\sum_i h_\delta((Ax-b)_i),\qquad
 \psi(x)=\tfrac12 x^TGx,\quad G\succ0,
\]
where $h_\delta'(a)=\operatorname{clip}(a,-\delta,\delta)$.
In whitened dual coordinates $t=G^{1/2}x$, put $B=AG^{-1/2}$. The map is
\begin{equation}
 \F(t)=t-\alpha B^T\operatorname{clip}(Bt-b,-\delta,\delta),
 \qquad 0<\alpha\leq 2/\norm{B}_2^2. \label{eq:huber}
\end{equation}
It is globally nonexpansive in Euclidean $t$-norm: the difference of two
updates is $(I-\alpha\overline B)(t-s)$, where the averaged Hessian obeys
$0\preceq\overline B\preceq\norm{B}_2^2 I$. Hence the eigenvalues of the
difference operator lie in $[-1,1]$. Fix an anchor $t$, residual $a=Bt-b$, and mask
$D_{ii}=1_{\{|a_i|<\delta\}}$. On the corresponding closed affine piece,
\[
 J=I-\alpha B^TDB,\qquad
 \F(t+v)=\F(t)+Jv.
\]
The projected path is $\widehat t_j=t+Qc_j$, where
$c_0=0$ and $c_{j+1}=\beta e_1+T_r c_j$.
Every piece constraint can be checked using only
\begin{equation}
 a_j=a+(BQ)c_j. \label{eq:featurepath}
\end{equation}
The columns $Bq_i$ are already computed by the tangent actions and cached.
The checks require feature-space operations, not another $B^T$ application
or another full map. Their execution time is nevertheless charged.

\begin{theorem}[An affine-piece discovery certificate]\label{thm:piece}
If \eqref{eq:featurepath} remains in the anchor's closed piece for every
input index $j=0,\ldots,H-1$, and $E=JQ-QT_r$, then
\begin{equation}
 \norm{\F^H(t)-(t+Qc_H)}_2
 \leq \sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\norm{Ec_j}_2. \label{eq:piecebound}
\end{equation}
In particular, algebraic closure $E=0$ makes the discovered finite
transport exact, even when its final output crosses a piece boundary.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The checked affine formula gives
$\F(\widehat t_j)-\widehat t_{j+1}=Ec_j$ at every predicted input.
For the actual state $t_j$, nonexpansivity gives
$\norm{t_{j+1}-\widehat t_{j+1}}
\leq\norm{t_j-\widehat t_j}+\norm{Ec_j}$.
Induction proves the bound. It is not necessary to assume that the
unknown actual path stays inside the same piece. The last checked point
is input $H-1$, not output $H$.
\end{proof}

The norms in \eqref{eq:piecebound} use the small Gram matrix $E^TE$.
This gives a computable path certificate in exact arithmetic and a
floating-point diagnostic subject to a stated numerical tolerance;
we do not claim directed-rounding certification. An exterior Euclidean
disk projection is curved, so this theorem does not transfer to Meyer
merely because its inside/outside mask stays unchanged.

\subsection{Unit-mode drift and the Anderson distinction}
If all Huber residuals are saturated, then $D=0$, $J=I$, and
$r=-\alpha B^T\operatorname{sign}(a)\delta$ is constant until the next event.
The first Arnoldi action discovers a rank-one cyclic space, and
$p_H(J)r=mr$. Put $d=Br$. For every feature moving toward its nearest
boundary, the continuous crossing time is
\[
 \tau_i=\frac{\operatorname{sign}(a_i)\delta-a_i}{d_i}>0.
\]
The horizon $H=1+\lfloor\min_i\tau_i\rfloor$ has all inputs in the current
closed piece and may place its output beyond the first boundary. A finite
cap limits acquisition amortization. In finite precision, each proposed
input must also be checked against its defining feature inequalities;
an uncertain boundary point cannot establish exact piece membership.
Thus the drift direction, one-dimensional relation, and first-event horizon
are all obtained from the current problem. They are not supplied by a
known optimum or by a future reference trajectory.

For type-II Anderson using a history entirely within this same constant-drift
piece, every residual difference is zero. With the minimum-norm or positive
ridge least-squares convention, its extrapolation coefficients are zero:
it takes one ordinary step. This is a precise collapse of acceleration,
not divergence of the underlying iteration. More generally, a left invariant direction $w^TJ=w^T$ with $w^Tr\ne0$
obstructs a stationary correction because $w^T(I-J)\delta=0$, while
$w^Tp_H(J)r=mw^Tr$ retains the required drift. A locally persistent pattern can therefore carry an unresolved future
event. History spanning other pieces,
roundoff, or alternative regularization can change the behavior; no universal
Anderson failure is asserted. The statement concerns this specified history and regularization convention.

\section{Curved Validity in the Coupled Meyer Map}\label{sec:curved}
The affine-piece certificate does not apply to an exterior disk projection.
Here we derive a different certificate from the original coupled recurrence,
retaining its curvature and both vector memories. The resulting construction
requires no additional screened solves during path validation.

\subsection{A faithful future-driving quotient and its metric}
Let $D$ be the periodic discrete gradient and $D^*=-\operatorname{div}$.
For a branch with $a=\eta/c>0$, define
\[
 S_a=(I+aD^*D)^{-1},\qquad R_r(t)=t-2\Pi_r(t),
\]
where $\Pi_r$ is the pointwise Euclidean disk projection. The exact stored
Meyer state is $(u,w,t_u,t_w)$. Its next pass depends on the primals only
through $s=u+w$:
\begin{align}
 u^+&=S_a\{s+aD^*R_{r_u}(t_u)\},\nonumber\\
 w^+&=S_b\{f-u^++bD^*R_{r_w}(t_w)\},\nonumber\\
 t_u^+&=Du^++\Pi_{r_u}(t_u),\nonumber\\
 t_w^+&=Dw^++\Pi_{r_w}(t_w),\qquad s^+=u^++w^+.
 \label{eq:meyerquotient}
\end{align}
The reported experiment sets $a=2$ and $b=10$. Thus
$z=(s,t_u,t_w)$ is an exact state for all future passes. Both vector fields,
including their transverse components, remain present. The current primal
difference $u-w$ is not recoverable from this quotient; the next actual pass
recovers both next primals exactly. The emitted texture $f-u-w=f-s$ is
already recoverable at the current state. This is a precise quotient of
redundant state, not deletion of a driving memory variable.

\begin{theorem}[Metric of the coupled recurrence]\label{thm:meyermetric}
The quotient map $F$ in \eqref{eq:meyerquotient} is nonexpansive in
\begin{equation}
 \norm{z}_M^2=\norm{s}_2^2+a\norm{t_u}_2^2+b\norm{t_w}_2^2.
 \label{eq:meyermetric}
\end{equation}
In fact, for any two states $z,\widetilde z$,
\begin{equation}
 \norm{Fz-F\widetilde z}_M^2+
 \tfrac12\norm{(I-F)z-(I-F)\widetilde z}_M^2
 \leq\norm{z-\widetilde z}_M^2. \label{eq:meyeraveraged}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, this map is $2/3$-averaged. The statement does not require
the input states to lie on an ordinary proximal trajectory.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
On pairs $(y,t)$ with norm $\norm{y}^2+a\norm{t}^2$, let $P_{\mathcal G_a}$
be the orthogonal projection onto the graph $\mathcal G_a=\{(x,Dx)\}$.
Its first component on $(y,R_r(t))$ is
$v=S_a(y+aD^*R_r(t))$. Therefore the branch map
\begin{align*}
 A_a(y,t)&=(v,Dv+\Pi_r(t))\\
 &=\tfrac12\{I+(2P_{\mathcal G_a}-I)\diag(I,R_r)\}(y,t)
\end{align*}
is firmly nonexpansive: graph reflection is an isometry, and $R_r$ is
nonexpansive by firm nonexpansivity of disk projection.

Write differences between two inputs and outputs with a $\Delta$; in the
following inequalities, $u,w$ denote the updated primals.
The first branch gives
\begin{align*}
 &\norm{\Delta u}^2+a\norm{\Delta t_u^+}^2\\
 &\quad\leq\norm{\Delta s}^2+a\norm{\Delta t_u}^2\\
 &\qquad-\norm{\Delta(s-u)}^2-a\norm{\Delta(t_u-t_u^+)}^2.
\end{align*}
Firm nonexpansivity of the second branch, whose input difference is
$(-\Delta u,\Delta t_w)$, gives
\[
 \norm{\Delta w}^2+b\norm{\Delta t_w^+}^2
 \leq-\ip{\Delta w}{\Delta u}+b\ip{\Delta t_w^+}{\Delta t_w}.
\]
Rearranging twice this inequality shows
\begin{align*}
 &\norm{\Delta(u+w)}^2+b\norm{\Delta t_w^+}^2\\
 &\quad\leq\norm{\Delta u}^2+b\norm{\Delta t_w}^2\\
 &\qquad-\norm{\Delta w}^2-b\norm{\Delta(t_w-t_w^+)}^2.
\end{align*}
Combining the two bounds proves nonexpansivity. Finally,
\[
 \norm{\Delta(s-u)}^2+\norm{\Delta w}^2
 \geq\tfrac12\norm{\Delta(s-u-w)}^2
\]
yields \eqref{eq:meyeraveraged} with the two vector-memory terms.
\end{proof}

Whitening this metric gives ordinary Euclidean Arnoldi on the five scalar
fields $(s,\sqrt a\,t_u,\sqrt b\,t_w)$. This five-field metric and a six-field Euclidean metric generally give
different projected polynomials. Exact state equivalence does not make
their compressed approximations identical.

\subsection{Exact algebraic description of disk curvature}
For an anchor vector $t=\rho n$, choose an orthonormal frame $(n,e)$ and
write a reduced displacement as $h=h_n n+h_t e$. Both $h_n$ and $h_t$ are
linear forms in the Krylov coordinates $c$. The new squared radius is
\begin{equation}
 \ell(c)^2=(\rho+h_n(c))^2+h_t(c)^2. \label{eq:diskradicand}
\end{equation}
It is a quadratic polynomial. The disk event is the inequality
$\ell(c)^2\leq r^2$; normalization introduces its positive square root.
These relations can be acquired once from the anchor and basis.

Let $q=\Pi_r(t+h)-\Pi_r(t)-P_t h$, where $P_t$ is the selected linear
projection action used by Arnoldi. For an interior anchor, including the
chosen identity action at $\rho=r$,
\begin{equation}
 \norm{q}^2=(\ell-r)_+^2. \label{eq:diskinside}
\end{equation}
For an exterior anchor $\rho>r$, put $\sigma=\min(1,r/\ell)$, with
$\sigma=1$ at $\ell=0$. Then
\begin{equation}
 q_n=\sigma(\rho+h_n)-r,\qquad
 q_t=(\sigma-r/\rho)h_t. \label{eq:diskoutside}
\end{equation}
These are exact identities on both sides of a crossing. In particular,
pure radial motion on the same exterior ray has zero remainder while
turning generally does not. A constant inside/outside mask alone fails to
capture this distinction. At nonsmooth boundary anchors, the argument uses
the specified linear action and exact remainder, not a nonexistent classical
Jacobian.

Only two linear forms per disk site are needed to evaluate
\eqref{eq:diskradicand}--\eqref{eq:diskoutside}. The proposed representation
is therefore a finite collection of polynomial radicands, branch inequalities,
and radial normalization relations. It does not claim global finite closure
of the nonlinear iteration in a polynomial observable dictionary.

\subsection{The nonlinear remainder has a fixed linear response}
Let $q_u,q_w$ be the exact projection remainders at a candidate displacement.
The corresponding remainder of the complete map has primal components
\begin{align}
 d_u&=-2aS_aD^*q_u,\nonumber\\
 d_w&=-S_b d_u-2bS_bD^*q_w,
 \label{eq:curveresponse}
\end{align}
and quotient components
$(d_u+d_w,Dd_u+q_u,Dd_w+q_w)$. Thus all nonlinear curvature is confined
to the two pointwise projection remainders; the rest is a fixed linear
response. We do not evaluate the screened solves in \eqref{eq:curveresponse}
for each predicted point. Instead, their operator norm supplies a bound.

For a discrete Laplacian eigenvalue $\lambda$, longitudinal components of
this response, after whitening, have Gram matrix
\begin{align*}
 G(\lambda)&=\begin{pmatrix}1-d(\lambda)&g(\lambda)\\g(\lambda)&1\end{pmatrix},\\
 d(\lambda)&=\frac{4a\lambda}{(1+a\lambda)^2(1+b\lambda)},\\
 g(\lambda)&=\frac{2\sqrt{ab}\lambda}{(1+a\lambda)(1+b\lambda)}.
\end{align*}
Transverse components have gain one. Consequently, with
\begin{equation}
 \gamma^2=\max_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(D^*D)}
 \left[1-\frac{d(\lambda)}2+
 \sqrt{\frac{d(\lambda)^2}4+g(\lambda)^2}\right], \label{eq:curvegain}
\end{equation}
we have
\begin{equation}
 \norm{N(v)}_M\leq
 \gamma\sqrt{a\norm{q_u(v)}_2^2+b\norm{q_w(v)}_2^2}.
 \label{eq:curvebound}
\end{equation}
This is a grid- and parameter-dependent calculation, independent of the
source image. For the reported $64\times64$ grid, $\gamma=1.14819$.
The Gram formula follows by Fourier diagonalization of
\eqref{eq:curveresponse}; the two transverse components pass unchanged.
Independent tests compare the full nonlinear remainder against the linear
response and its norm bound.

\begin{theorem}[Curved Meyer path certificate]\label{thm:curvedpath}
For an $M$-orthonormal Arnoldi basis, let $E=JQ-QT_r$,
$c_0=0$, $c_{j+1}=\beta e_1+T_r c_j$, and
$\widehat z_j=z+Qc_j$. Define
\[
 \varepsilon_j=\norm{Ec_j}_M+
 \gamma\sqrt{a\norm{q_u(Qc_j)}^2+b\norm{q_w(Qc_j)}^2}.
\]
Then, for every finite horizon,
\begin{equation}
 \norm{F^H(z)-\widehat z_H}_M
 \leq\sum_{j=0}^{H-1}\varepsilon_j. \label{eq:curvedpath}
\end{equation}
The same bound controls the emitted texture error. It also remains valid
after a common ordinary settling step. No fixed-mask assumption is required.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The exact defect is $N(Qc_j)+Ec_j$. Apply \eqref{eq:curvebound} and the
triangle inequality, then propagate errors by Theorem~\ref{thm:meyermetric}.
The initial error is zero, so summation gives \eqref{eq:curvedpath}.
Texture is $f-s$ and its error norm is bounded by the full quotient norm.
A common settling step cannot increase the quotient error.
\end{proof}

All projection remainders along the predicted path are evaluated; this is
not sparse sampling of a few convenient points. The reduction is algebraic:
small-coordinate evaluation and pointwise radial operations replace repeated
screened solves. Compression norms use the small matrix $E^TME$.
The certificate is proved in exact arithmetic and tested in floating point;
we do not claim outward-rounded numerical certification.


\section{Projective Sinkhorn Closure}\label{app:projective}
\begin{proposition}[Two-by-two Sinkhorn lift]
\label{prop:sinkhorn-projective}
For $K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p&q\\r&s\end{smallmatrix}\right)>0$,
the quotient $t=v_1/v_2$ obeys $t^+=(At+B)/(Ct+D)$, where
\begin{align*}
 A&=(b_1/b_2)(q a_1r+s a_2p),&
 B&=(b_1/b_2)qs(a_1+a_2),\\
 C&=pr(a_1+a_2),&D&=p a_1s+r a_2q.
\end{align*}
With $\mathsf L=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
\begin{equation}\begin{aligned}
 t_H&=\frac{(\mathsf L^H)_{11}t_0+(\mathsf L^H)_{12}}
              {(\mathsf L^H)_{21}t_0+(\mathsf L^H)_{22}},\\
 \frac{dt_H}{dt_0}&=
 \frac{\det(\mathsf L)^H}{\{(\mathsf L^H)_{21}t_0+(\mathsf L^H)_{22}\}^2}.
\end{aligned}\label{eq:sinkhorn-projective-power}\end{equation}
If $ps\ne qr$, the one-pass derivative
$\det(\mathsf L)/(Ct+D)^2$ is nonconstant in $t$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
In any scale gauge, insert
$u_1=a_1/(pt+q)$ and $u_2=a_2/(rt+s)$ into
$t^+=(b_1/b_2)(qu_1+su_2)/(pu_1+ru_2)$ and clear denominators.
Composition of fractional-linear maps multiplies their homogeneous
matrices. Differentiating the resulting fraction proves the second formula;
$\det(\mathsf L)=(b_1/b_2)a_1a_2(ps-qr)^2$.
\end{proof}

Cayley--Hamilton expresses $\mathsf L^H=\alpha_H\mathsf L+\beta_HI$, and normalized binary
powering needs $O(\log H)$ fixed-size matrix products. This transports a
changing physical derivative using a constant lifted operator. Exact
rational bit lengths and floating-point conditioning still require attention.
The lift describes quotient states. To recover the ordinary post-column
coupling after $H$ passes, advance through $H-1$ and perform the final
row/column pair in a consistent gauge; a row-normalized output is obtained by evaluating the next row
normalization.

The closure extends to arbitrary dimensions with supplied exact structure
\begin{equation}
 K_{ij}=r_i C_{g(i),h(j)}s_j.
 \label{eq:sinkhorn-block-kernel}
\end{equation}
Let $A_g=\sum_{i:g(i)=g}a_i$, $B_h=\sum_{j:h(j)=h}b_j$.
After one full pass, $v_j=(b_j/s_j)w_{h(j)}/B_{h(j)}$, and substitution
gives the invariant reduced map
\begin{equation}
 w^+=B\big/\{C^T(A/(Cw))\}.
 \label{eq:sinkhorn-block-map}
\end{equation}
For arbitrary initial $v$, use $w_h=\sum_{j:h(j)=h}s_jv_j$ for the entry
pass. Two groups on each side reduce exactly to
Proposition~\ref{prop:sinkhorn-projective}. Within-manifold derivative
evolution follows the same lift; arbitrary initial perturbations also
require the derivative of the entry map. Discovery or verification of the
block structure must be charged, and rank two alone does not imply
\eqref{eq:sinkhorn-block-kernel}.


\section{Gaussian Precision Convention}\label{app:gaussian}
Take the continuous Lebesgue kernel
$K(x,y)=\exp(-\norm{x-y}^2/(2t))$ and centered Gaussian marginal
densities with covariance matrices $A,B\succ0$. If the column scaling has
quadratic exponent $-y^TVy/2$, write $p=t^{-1}I+V\succ0$.
Completing the square in $Kv$ gives a row exponent whose precision is
$t^{-1}I-t^{-2}p^{-1}$. Dividing the source density by that integral
therefore gives row-scaling precision
\[
 U=A^{-1}-t^{-1}I+t^{-2}p^{-1}.
\]
The next column integral has conditional precision
$t^{-1}I+U=A^{-1}+t^{-2}p^{-1}\succ0$.
Dividing the target density by it yields
\begin{align*}
 p^+&=B^{-1}+t^{-2}(A^{-1}+t^{-2}p^{-1})^{-1}\\
    &=B^{-1}+(t^2A^{-1}+p^{-1})^{-1},
\end{align*}
which proves \eqref{eq:gaussian}. The scaling functions themselves need
not be integrable separately; the stated conditional integrals are.
Scalar normalization constants do not affect this covariance recurrence.

The reported two-dimensional controls include isotropic, commuting
anisotropic, and rotated covariance pairs at four regularization values.
The isotropic $t=.001$ control agrees with ordinary precision iteration
at horizon 2048 to approximately $3.4\cdot10^{-14}$ relative error.
Generic block powering is not uniformly stable in the anisotropic controls:
ill-conditioned projective denominators can spoil finite-precision readout.
Exact Riccati closure is therefore a structural example, not a claim that
this generic implementation is a robust accelerator for every covariance.

\section{Experimental Comparison Conditions}\label{app:protocol}
Each experiment uses its own matched ordinary control; kernels and problem
setup are shared or excluded consistently within that experiment.
Future ordinary trajectories, dense spectra, and converged fixed points
are used only for scoring unless explicitly labeled as supplied oracle
information. Analytic certificates are exact-arithmetic results checked in
float64; no interval-arithmetic implementation is claimed.

\subsection{Discovery and supplied structure}
The Huber complete-solve target is objective-gap ratio $10^{-3}$, checked
every sixteen work units; the constructed noiseless inverse problem has
known zero optimum. Three seeds and three shuffled repeats include failed
acquisitions, checks, and fallback steps. At $n=256$, the 3.47 ratio is the
median of paired per-seed speed ratios. The Anderson implementation is an
unguarded regularized type-II control, not a faithful reproduction of
AA-BPG. Its better complete time does not certify reproduction of the
ordinary intermediate trajectory.

The supplied-block Sinkhorn study uses sizes 256--8192 and coupling values
$.03,.001$, with both methods operating on the same reduced map.
Full-scaling readout is included. Its stopping test is maximum relative
column error $10^{-9}$ after exact row normalization. Five repeats after
warm-up are used; the reported tables contain 120 structured solves.
The stated 56.9--73.8 range concerns only the $.001$ family.

The Meyer coverage study uses five $64\times64$ sources and four ordinary
anchor prefixes, with depths $2,4,8$ and horizons $8,16,32,64$. All 240
path comparisons satisfy the bound within numerical tolerance.
The live policy accepts seven of twenty anchors; three accepted blocks
still lose time. Complete solves use the same recovered primal--dual gap,
target $10^{-4}$ of its initial value, and checks every 32 work units.
Discovery and all rejections are charged. All five complete solves finish;
all five discovered runs lose against ordinary iteration.

\subsection{Primitive trajectories and family controls}
The discrete transport instances use independent point clouds
$X_i,Y_j\in[0,1]^2$ sampled uniformly. Set
$C_{ij}=\norm{X_i-Y_j}_2^2/\max_{k,l}\norm{X_k-Y_l}_2^2$ and
$K_{ij}=\exp(-C_{ij}/\varepsilon)$.
The source and target masses are separately normalized samples of
$w(z)=.05+\sum_{\ell}\exp(-\norm{z-c_\ell}_2^2/.02)$,
with two independently uniform centers for the source and three for the
target. The six cache comparisons combine two independent instances with
$\varepsilon\in\{.01,.003,.001\}$; all start from unit column scaling
and allow at most 32 stored response directions.
Primitive-cache comparisons use 256 complete passes from the same initial
scaling, with three repeats and single-threaded dense linear algebra. The
per-query exact-arithmetic admission threshold is $10^{-8}$.
At $n=1024$ the six final Hilbert log-state errors are
$1.37\cdot10^{-8}$--$3.18\cdot10^{-8}$; these are errors relative to the
ordinary finite trajectory, not marginal residuals or objective gaps.
The four speedups and two regressions in Table~\ref{tab:regimes} refer
to these six matched finite trajectories.

The mixed-algebra family control fixes the log-coefficient RMS scale at
1.4 and uses one, two, or four standardized fields, binary or Gaussian.
Supplied generators are distinguished from SVD generators learned after
eight live requests. The response budget is 64 measured columns.
Maximum observed Hilbert output error is
$1.13\cdot10^{-8}$ and maximum observed bound excess is
$9.46\cdot10^{-14}$. At $n=128$ the mixed methods take approximately
6--13 ms against $.6$ ms ordinary evaluation despite favorable binary
product counts.

The difficult-trajectory comparison uses the same random point and mass
construction at $n=1024$, regularization $.001$, two independent instances,
a budget of 64 response columns, and three timed repeats.
Its median ordinary times are 73 and 61 ms; two-direction mixed times are
216 and 217 ms, with retrospective rejection diagnostics included.
The product-count failure does not depend on those diagnostic costs.
The largest new-carrier terminal Hilbert error is $2.13\cdot10^{-10}$;
most requests are evaluated by ordinary fallback.

\subsection{Proof checks and scope of the evidence}
Independent numerical checks cover mirror symmetry, affine Arnoldi
