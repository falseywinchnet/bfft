English translation — Block 6, continued

[Audience member:] “The Holy One, blessed be He, forgives.”

Everyone will receive what he deserves. Whoever repents will be saved. I just want to finish with one last minute. You know that it is written in the Torah that it is forbidden—absolutely forbidden—to take a bribe. What does “forbidden to take a bribe” mean? Not that if you take a bribe you will be obligated to help the person who gave you money. That is obvious; any fool understands it. You do not need the Torah for that. Do non-Jews in China not understand that if a judge takes money from Reuven, he will have to rule in his favor? What need is there for the Torah to say that? Everyone understands.

Rather, it does not say that after receiving money you will now be obligated to the briber. No. It says that a bribe “blinds the eyes of the wise and perverts the words of the righteous.” It blinds the judge. The judge no longer sees the true judgment. He cannot see fault in the person who gave him a gift; he likes him. A bond of gratitude forms between them. He cannot rule against him, because the man gave him a gift.

Suppose after the trial the FBI comes to the judge and says, “Come into this room for a lie-detector test. We want to see whether you perverted the judgment.” He passes without a problem. “What? I perverted justice? I ruled exactly according to the evidence.” Then they examine things and see that he passes the test even though he took a bribe. The bribe apparently blinded him; he no longer sees that the person who paid him is guilty. In his mind, that person is innocent.

It is like among the ultra-Orthodox. If some great rabbi does something that should not be done, anyone who loves him, learned from him, and was strengthened by him immediately runs to defend him. He does not know the details. Perhaps the prosecution is correct. Nobody knows the details—not those for him and not those against him. Those who value and admire him immediately give him endless defenses; those who hate him immediately prosecute him. Neither side knows the details. So why run and fight about something when you do not know the details? See the whole investigation, all the evidence, the testimony; sit in the court and then understand. Why not? Because you are biased. You love him; he helped you, hosted you, brought you to repentance, taught you Torah, helped your child enter a yeshiva. You cannot see fault in him.

That is what the Torah means. Once you have received even a small benefit—even a greeting with a smile from someone—you can no longer be his judge. He lives on your street; he prays in your synagogue; you can no longer judge him. Why? “If I convict him, everyone here in the community will be angry.” How important this is.

But a non-Jew once asked a rabbi: “Look, with us, the one who gives a bribe and the one who receives a bribe are both punished; both are criminals. How is it that in your Torah there is only a warning not to receive a bribe? It does not say not to give one. Only the receiver is warned. Why does the Torah not say, ‘Be careful not to give a bribe, not to bribe’?” He told the rabbi, “You see? Your religion has problems. The Torah says only not to accept a bribe.”

The rabbi told him, “Listen well: the Holy One, blessed be He, never makes mistakes. If both the bribe-taker and bribe-giver were criminals and both were punished, you would never catch either one. Why? The one receiving the bribe relies on the fact that the giver will also go to prison, so of course he will not inform on me. If he informs on me, he falls with me. His interest is that I am never caught, because on the day I am caught he falls with me. I will protect him and he will protect me, and no one will ever know.

“But if the giver has nothing to fear and only the receiver is the criminal, one day the giver—who did violate ‘You shall not place a stumbling block before the blind,’ because he caused someone to violate a Torah prohibition—may wake up, want to repent, come and confess: ‘I gave that judge money.’ Since he has nothing to fear, he can one day repent and admit that he gave a bribe. Then I, the receiver, am never safe. All my life I must live in fear: one day he will turn on me.

“What did the Holy One do? He created a reality in which, if you take a bribe, you live in fear all your life, because one day the giver may come confess and has nothing to fear. They will do nothing to him; on the contrary, they will say, ‘Thank you for coming and telling the truth.’ So now the judge will think a thousand times before taking the bribe. Do you understand? The giver has nothing to lose; he will come and bring me down. He will bring me down.”

Notice: in the end the Torah is always right. There are only people here who think that they know better than God.

With God’s help, may it be His will that we pass this month of Elul successfully, with genuine repentance, and that on Rosh Hashanah the Holy One, blessed be He, decree good decrees for us. You know, this is a very dangerous year. Any year in which Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat is catastrophic, because the shofar is not blown on Shabbat. That is how it was on October 7, how it was in the Holocaust, and how it was in many situations. Before tragedies came—in the coronavirus period too—it was like this. Before great tragedies come to the world and the people, Rosh Hashanah always falls on Shabbat and it is impossible to blow the shofar. That is what is written in the Talmud.

Therefore, from now until Rosh Hashanah, we must cry to God twice as much, do kindness twice as much, increase Torah, increase repentance, and increase bringing merit to the public, in order to sweeten the judgments. Thank you very much for the organization and hospitality. May God bless you with good news. Blessed is the Lord forever, amen and amen.
